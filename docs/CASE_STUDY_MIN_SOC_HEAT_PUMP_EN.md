## 1. System Baseline & Operating Conditions

* **Inverter:** 3-phase hybrid inverter GoodWe ET series (10 kW).
* **Battery Stack:** High-voltage battery stack with **~15 kWh nominal capacity** (charge efficiency $\eta_{ch} = 90\%$, discharge efficiency $\eta_{dis} = 90\%$).
* **Electricity Tariff:** Multi-zone Time-of-Use TOU / G13 schedule:
  * **07:00 – 13:00:** Mid-peak morning zone **T1** (**0.90 PLN/kWh**).
  * **13:00 – 16:00:** Off-peak afternoon valley **T3** (**0.62 PLN/kWh**).
  * **16:00 – 21:00:** Critical expensive evening peak **T2** (**1.49 PLN/kWh**).
* **Weather & Operating Environment:** Overcast late-November day, near-freezing outside temperature driving high heat pump demand, total PV generation negligible at **~35 W**. The battery operates in a comfortable indoor utility room (cell temperatures well within optimal limits: 23–28°C).
* **Household Consumption:** Heat pump compressor modulation ramp-up plus baseline house loads (combined draw: **~3.8 kW**).
* **Active EMS Thermal Guard:** Dynamic reserve limit calculated by EMS: **`Dynamic Min SoC = 40%`** (winter regime: increased blackout reserve during heating season and providing a reliable baseline for pre-peak afternoon replenishment).

## 3. The Mathematics of EMHASS Planning: Why 89% Instead of 100%?

An intuitive yet mistaken assumption is that an off-peak valley should "always charge the battery to 100%". The actual LP plan generated by EMHASS at 12:00 for the subsequent periods demonstrated mathematical sizing:

### Trajectory Profile from the Solver:
* **12:00 – 13:00 (T1 Zone @ 0.90 PLN):** Battery drains to the Min SoC threshold of **40.0%** (then enters standby).
* **13:00 – 16:00 (Off-peak Valley T3 @ 0.62 PLN):** Planned grid charge at a steady, moderate **$P_{ch} = 2700\text{ W}$**.
  * Over the 3-hour window, net energy stored is:
    $$E_{in} = 2.7\text{ kW} \times 3\text{ h} \times 0.90\text{ (efficiency)} = \mathbf{7.29\text{ kWh}}$$
  * Corresponding SoC rise:
    $$\Delta\text{SoC} = \frac{7.29\text{ kWh}}{15.12\text{ kWh}} \approx \mathbf{48.2\%}$$
  * Pre-peak state at 16:00 reaches:
    $$\text{SoC}_{16:00} = 40.85\% + 48.22\% = \mathbf{89.07\%}$$
* **16:00 – 21:00 (Critical Peak T2 @ 1.49 PLN):**
  * Total projected household consumption (heat pump + domestic loads) equals approximately **$6.6\text{ kWh}$** (1.2–1.5 kW per 30-min step).
  * Discharging from **89.07%** down to **40.0%** releases:
    $$E_{out} = (89.07\% - 40.0\%) \times 15.12\text{ kWh} \times 0.90 = \mathbf{6.68\text{ kWh}}$$
  * At **20:30–21:00**, battery SoC reaches **exactly 40.0% (Min SoC)** precisely as the expensive T2 peak concludes!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Optimal Target Sizing:**  
> The solver **does not overcharge to 100%**. It charges precisely to **89%**—the exact energy required to cover the expensive T2 evening peak (16:00–21:00) down to `Min SoC = 40%`.  
> Charging beyond 89% would:
> 1. Force higher grid import power between 13:00 and 16:00 (risking connection limits while the heat pump is running).
> 2. Lock up unnecessary capital in the battery before the next cheap overnight valley.

## 4. Key Engineering Takeaways

1. **Winter Emergency & Blackout Reserve:** Preserving 40% (~6 kWh) during the heating season guarantees continuous backup for central heating controllers, hydronic circulating pumps, network infrastructure, and critical home circuits during grid outages.
2. **Deterministic Baseline:** Halting at 40% gave the optimization engine a clean, stable foundation. Sizing a 2.7 kW charge over 3 hours ensured an 89% state at 16:00 without grid stress.
3. **Smooth Transition (Zero Flapping):** Shifted 3.8 kW from battery to grid in 5 seconds with deadband stability.
